Stars don’t last for ever, so they must be born, and they still are: some of the stars in Orion are younger than our species. This chapter follows a star from a cold, dark cloud to its arrival on the main sequence (Chapter 13). It also meets the ones that never make it, and finds out why the sky is full of small stars and has so few big ones.

## The space between the stars

The space between the stars isn’t empty. It holds the <Term id="interstellar-medium">interstellar medium</Term>: gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, at about one atom per cubic centimetre on average. That is a far better vacuum than any laboratory can make, but over light-years it adds up. Some of it is hot, near a million degrees, blown out by exploding stars; some is warm, at about 8,000 K; some is gathered into cold clouds.

About 1% of its mass is dust: grains of rock and soot smaller than a micrometre, made in the winds of dying stars. Dust blocks light, and it blocks short wavelengths most. Starlight that passes through dust comes out fainter and redder, just as the Sun looks red through haze at sunset, though for a different reason. In 1930 Robert Trumpler found that distant star clusters looked fainter than their sizes said they should: dust was dimming them, by about one or two magnitudes for every thousand parsecs along the Milky Way’s disc. Towards the centre of our Galaxy the dust dims visible light by a factor of a trillion, but infrared light gets through. That is why infrared telescopes can see right to the centre.

Where dust is thickest it hides the stars behind it completely. These dark clouds were once thought to be holes in the sky, until E. E. Barnard catalogued them and argued that they were clouds in front of the stars. Barnard 68, a small one about 400 light-years away, blots out every star behind it in visible light; in infrared, the stars shine through. And where a hot young star sits inside a cloud, its ultraviolet light strips the electrons off the hydrogen around it, and the gas glows red with hydrogen’s line, Hα: Kirchhoff’s bright lines (Chapter 12). The Orion Nebula, 1,300 light-years away, glows like that around its young stars.

## Clouds that collapse

Stars are born in the coldest places in the Galaxy. <Term id="molecular-cloud">Molecular clouds</Term> are at 10 to 20 degrees above absolute zero, cold enough for hydrogen atoms to pair up into molecules, and dense, with hundreds to millions of molecules per cubic centimetre. Hydrogen molecules give out almost no light at those temperatures, so astronomers trace the clouds by their dust and by carbon monoxide, which shines in radio waves.

A cloud is pulled together by its own gravity and pushed apart by its heat: its molecules dart about, and the faster they go, the harder they push. Gravity wins if the cloud is heavy enough. James Jeans worked out the dividing line in 1902; above it, in the cloud’s <Term id="jeans-mass">Jeans mass</Term>, nothing stops the collapse. Cold gas pushes back weakly, and dense gas pulls hard, so the colder and denser a cloud, the smaller its Jeans mass. That is why stars form in the coldest, densest clouds.

<Callout kind="maths">

**The Jeans mass.** A cloud collapses if it is heavier than M<sub>J</sub> ∝ T<sup>3/2</sup> / √ρ, where T is its temperature and ρ its density. Halve the temperature and the Jeans mass falls by a factor of 2<sup>3/2</sup> ≈ 2.8; make the cloud 100 times denser and it falls by 10. For molecular gas at 10 K with 10,000 molecules per cubic centimetre it is about 5 solar masses, and a cloud like that falls in on itself in about 340,000 years, its free-fall time.

A giant molecular cloud is thousands of times heavier than its Jeans mass, so it doesn’t collapse as one. As it falls in, it gets denser, its Jeans mass shrinks, and it breaks into clumps, and the clumps into smaller ones. That is why stars are born in groups and clusters, from dozens to many thousands at once. Only a few per cent of a cloud’s gas ends up in stars: the newborn stars’ light and winds blow the rest away.

## From protostar to star

Each collapsing knot makes a <Term id="protostar">protostar</Term>. As its gas falls inwards, it speeds up and heats: half of the energy it gains from falling heats the gas, and the other half is shone away. The protostar is wrapped in a cocoon of dust, so it can be seen only in infrared, glowing from the heat of its own squeezing. Gas keeps falling onto it for a few hundred thousand years, mostly through a spinning disc, the same kind of disc in which planets form (Chapter 8). Some of the gas is flung out again along the spin axis in two narrow jets, which light up the gas they hit: George Herbig and Guillermo Haro found these glowing knots in 1951–52.

Once the cocoon clears, the young star can be seen. Stars of about the Sun’s mass are then <Term id="t-tauri-star">T Tauri stars</Term>, named after the first one Alfred Joy described in 1945: flickering, still with their discs, and still shrinking. Nothing in them is fusing hydrogen yet; they shine on the heat of their own contraction, as Kelvin thought the Sun did (Chapter 15). As they shrink their middles heat up, until at about 10 million degrees hydrogen starts to fuse. The new heat holds the star up, the shrinking stops, and the star has arrived on the main sequence.

On the HR diagram, young stars come down from the upper right. The smallest come almost straight down, shrinking at nearly the same temperature, because they are convective all the way through, their heat carried by rising and sinking gas right down to the centre: Chushiro Hayashi worked out these tracks in 1961. Stars like the Sun then turn left, heating up at nearly the same brightness, once convection stops in their middles and light takes over carrying the heat there. The heavier the star, the faster it arrives: a star like the Sun takes about 40 million years, a red dwarf of a tenth of the Sun’s mass hundreds of millions, and the heaviest stars are on the main sequence while gas is still falling onto them.

## Failed stars

What if a ball of gas is too small? As it shrinks, the electrons in its middle get squeezed so tightly that they resist being squeezed further, whatever the temperature; physicists call this degeneracy pressure, and it is what holds up white dwarfs (Chapter 18). If that pressure stops the shrinking before the middle reaches the temperature for fusing hydrogen, fusion never gets going for good. The object is a <Term id="brown-dwarf">brown dwarf</Term>.

The dividing line is about 0.075 times the Sun’s mass, or 78 times Jupiter’s, for stars made like the Sun; for stars poorer in heavy elements it is a little higher. Brown dwarfs do fuse a little: above about 13 Jupiter masses they burn their small store of deuterium, heavy hydrogen, for a while, and the heaviest burn their lithium. But with no lasting fuel they only cool, fading for ever and passing through the spectral classes M, L, T and, below about 500 K, Y.

## Many small stars, few big ones

A cloud doesn’t make stars of every mass equally. In 1955 Edwin Salpeter counted stars near the Sun, corrected for the big ones that have already died, and found that with each doubling of mass, about two and a half times fewer stars are born. Later counts, such as Pavel Kroupa’s in 2001, found that the rule flattens below about half the Sun’s mass, but small stars still win: this <Term id="initial-mass-function">initial mass function</Term> seems to be much the same wherever stars are born.

The result is a population of small stars. Only about one star in ten is born heavier than the Sun, and only one in 160 is heavier than 8 Suns, the stars that will end as supernovae. The average newborn star has about 0.6 of the Sun’s mass. But the few big stars matter far more than their numbers suggest: a star of 20 Suns shines about 40,000 times as brightly as the Sun, so a young cluster’s light comes almost all from its heaviest members. Why does nature make so many small stars? It isn’t fully understood. Clouds break into clumps of many sizes; small clumps are common, and big stars can grow only by gathering more gas before their own light blows it away. Too small, and fusion never starts; too big, and a star’s light pushes its own outer layers off. Between those limits, stars are born from about a thirteenth of the Sun’s mass to a couple of hundred times it, and Chapter 17 follows what happens to them next.
