Astronomy is the science of big numbers. Before we take the sky's clockwork apart, we need a feel for how big the machine is, and that is harder than it sounds: the distances out there are so large that every picture you have seen of the Solar System has had to lie about them. This chapter shows them true.

## Ten times bigger, again and again

Start with yourself, a little under 2 metres long. Now picture a square 10 m across, then 100 m, then 1,000 m. Each step makes the view ten times wider. After 7 steps the view is 10,000 km across, a little less than the width of Earth. After 13, the whole family of planets fits. After 21 steps, the view holds the entire Milky Way: a few hundred billion stars.

Numbers like that get unwieldy. Writing 1,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 m is no fun, and it's easy to miscount the zeros. So scientists write it as 10²¹ m, "ten to the twenty-one": a 1 followed by 21 zeros, or 21 steps of ×10. A number that isn't a neat power of ten gets a number in front: Earth is 12,742 km across, which is 1.27 × 10⁷ m. This is <Term id="scientific-notation">scientific notation</Term>, and the small raised number is simply a count of steps on the zoom. The Toolkit page [Powers of ten](/toolkit/powers-of-ten/) shows how to calculate with it.

Notice what the zoom keeps doing. Every few steps there is a cluster of things, and then a long stretch of nothing. Earth and the Moon, then empty space. The Sun and its planets, then empty space for thousands of times their size. Stars, then more empty space. That emptiness is the first fact about the universe, and the hardest to picture.

<Callout>

Each step of ×10 makes a view ten times wider, so it covers a hundred times the area. Twenty-one steps take you from a person to a galaxy. On a scale that wide, "big" and "small" stop meaning much; what matters is *how many steps* apart two things are.

</Callout>

<DeepDive title="The film that did this first">

The zoom is an old idea. In 1977 Charles and Ray Eames made a short film, *Powers of Ten*, that starts with a couple having a picnic in a Chicago park and pulls back by a factor of ten every ten seconds, to the edge of the known universe, then dives into a hand, down to the scale of a proton. If you've never seen it, it's worth nine minutes. This course stops at the Milky Way: the universe beyond it is a subject for another course.

</DeepDive>

## A Solar System on a football pitch

The zoom shows the sizes and distances honestly, but it can only show one scale at a time. A scale model shows them all together. Shrink everything by the same factor, and see what you get.

Put the Sun on the centre spot of a football pitch as a grapefruit, 10 cm across. That shrinks everything by about 14 billion times. Then:

- **Earth** is a grain of sand, 0.9 mm across, 10.8 m from the grapefruit: just outside the centre circle. The **Moon** is a speck a quarter of a millimetre across, 2.8 cm from it.
- **Jupiter**, the biggest planet, is a pea 56 m away, just past the goal line.
- **Neptune**, the farthest planet, is a small peppercorn 323 m away, three pitches off.
- The nearest other star, **Proxima Centauri**, is a cherry about 2,900 km away: in another country.

Between them there is almost nothing: a grapefruit and eight grains and seeds, scattered over a patch of ground more than half a kilometre wide, and the next grapefruit a long-haul flight away.

<Callout kind="note" title="Why the pictures lie">

Diagrams of the Solar System in books, on posters and on screens squeeze the planets close together and blow them up so you can see them. They have to: drawn to true scale on a page, the Sun would be a dot and every planet invisible. But the lie sticks. Many people picture the Solar System as a compact, crowded place, with the asteroid belt as a field of rocks to dodge. In fact spacecraft fly straight through the belt without steering round anything. Whenever a figure in this course has to exaggerate, it carries a **Not to scale** tag.

</Callout>

## Light as a measuring stick

Kilometres run out of meaning quickly. A better yardstick for space is the fastest thing there is. Light travels at 299,792,458 metres per second, about 300,000 km every second, and nothing goes faster. So astronomers often measure a distance by how long light takes to cross it:

| Light takes | To cross | Which is |
|---|---|---|
| 1.28 seconds | from the Moon to Earth | 384,400 km |
| 8 minutes 19 seconds | from the Sun to Earth | 150 million km, or 1 AU |
| about 4 hours 10 minutes | from the Sun to Neptune | 30 AU |
| a day | from Earth to Voyager 1 | 26 billion km |
| 4.25 years | from Proxima Centauri to us | 40 trillion km |

The second row gives the Solar System's own unit of distance: the <Term id="au">astronomical unit</Term>, or AU, the average distance between Earth and the Sun. And the last row gives the unit for the stars: the <Term id="light-year">light-year</Term>, the distance light travels in a year, about 9.46 trillion km. A light-year is a distance, not a time.

There is a strange side to measuring with light. Light takes time to arrive, so you never see anything as it is now. You see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago, and Proxima Centauri as it was 4 years ago. Looking out into space is looking back in time.

<Callout kind="know">

How do we know these distances so precisely? Inside the Solar System, by timing radio waves. Since 1961, astronomers have bounced radar off Venus and timed spacecraft signals, which turns the speed of light into distances good to metres. The astronomical unit was fixed in 2012 at exactly 149,597,870,700 m. Distances to the stars come from a different trick, parallax, which Chapter 9 explains.

</Callout>
