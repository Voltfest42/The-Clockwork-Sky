Johannes Kepler was a mathematician with poor eyesight and a mystic’s conviction that the Solar System was built on a hidden plan. He inherited the best observations anyone had ever made, Tycho Brahe’s, and spent years forcing himself to take them seriously. What came out were three short laws that describe every orbit in the Solar System, and that Newton would later explain.

## Mars refuses a circle

Kepler started from the Sun-centred system and set out to find Mars’s orbit from Tycho’s observations. Like everyone before him, he assumed it was a circle, and like Ptolemy he allowed himself an off-centre circle and an equant. After years of calculation he had a model that matched Tycho’s positions of Mars at opposition to within two minutes of arc. It was better than anything before. But when he tested it on other observations, it was off by up to 8 minutes of arc: about a quarter of the Moon’s width.

Before Tycho, nobody would have noticed; Ptolemy’s and Copernicus’s data were only good to about 10 minutes of arc. But Kepler knew Tycho’s measurements were good to one or two. Those eight minutes, he wrote, could not be ignored, and they alone pointed the way to reforming the whole of astronomy. He threw the circle away.

After more false starts, he found the shape that fitted: an <Term id="ellipse">ellipse</Term>, with the Sun not at its centre but at one of two special points inside it, the foci (each one a <Term id="focus">focus</Term>). This is Kepler’s first law, published in *Astronomia nova*, the “New Astronomy”, in 1609:

<Callout kind="key">

**Kepler’s first law.** Each planet moves on an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.

</Callout>

An ellipse is easy to draw. Push two pins into a board, loop a piece of string round them, pull it tight with a pencil and go round: the string keeps the pencil’s distances to the two pins adding up to the same total. The pins are the foci. The farther apart they are, the more stretched the ellipse; how stretched it is is called its <Term id="eccentricity">eccentricity</Term>, which runs from 0, a circle, towards 1, a long thin oval.

The planets’ orbits are very nearly circles. Earth’s has an eccentricity of 0.017: drawn to fill this page, it would differ from a circle by less than the width of the line. Only the Sun’s position gives it away, set slightly off-centre, so that Earth is about 147 million km from the Sun in early January and 152 million km in early July. Mars’s orbit, at 0.093, is the most stretched of the planets Tycho could watch apart from Mercury’s, which is why it gave the game away. Comets are different: Halley’s comet, at 0.97, swings in to just over half Earth’s distance from the Sun and out beyond Neptune.

The <Term id="semi-major-axis">semi-major axis</Term> is half the ellipse’s longest diameter. It is also the average of the planet’s nearest and farthest distances from the Sun, its <Term id="perihelion">perihelion</Term> and <Term id="aphelion">aphelion</Term>, so it is the natural measure of the size of an orbit: for Earth, 1 AU.

## Equal areas in equal times

A planet on an ellipse doesn’t move at a steady speed. Kepler found the rule, in fact before he found the ellipse: imagine a line from the Sun to the planet. As the planet moves, the line sweeps across the inside of the orbit, and it sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

<Callout kind="key">

**Kepler’s second law.** The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

</Callout>

Near the Sun the line is short, so to sweep the same area in a day the planet has to cover a longer stretch of its orbit: it moves fast. Far from the Sun the line is long, and a shorter stretch does: it moves slowly.

Earth moves at about 30.3 km/s at perihelion in early January and 29.3 km/s at aphelion in early July, which is why the Sun drifts east among the stars a little faster in January than in July (Chapter 3), and why the northern winter, from the December solstice to the March equinox, is the shortest season, nearly five days shorter than the northern summer. Mercury varies between 59 and 39 km/s. Halley’s comet races past the Sun at about 55 km/s and creeps round the far end of its orbit, beyond Neptune, at under 1 km/s, which is why it spends most of its 76 years far out.

Why should the line sweep equal areas? Kepler guessed that a force from the Sun drives the planets, weaker farther away. He was half right. Newton showed that the second law holds for any force that pulls straight towards the Sun, whatever its strength. It is the conservation of angular momentum, the same rule that makes a spinning skater speed up as they pull their arms in.

## The harmonic law

Kepler’s first two laws describe one planet at a time. His third connects them all. He searched for it for a decade, and found it on 15 May 1618; he published it the next year in *Harmonices mundi*, “The Harmony of the World”. It links the time a planet takes to go round the Sun, its period, with the size of its orbit.

Measure the period P in years and the size of the orbit (the semi-major axis) a in AU. Then:

<Callout kind="key">

**Kepler’s third law.** P × P = a × a × a. The square of the period equals the cube of the size.

</Callout>

Earth: 1 × 1 = 1 × 1 × 1. Jupiter is 5.2 AU from the Sun; 5.2 × 5.2 × 5.2 is 141, and the square root of 141 is 11.9: Jupiter takes 11.9 years to go round, as it does. A planet 4 AU from the Sun would have a year of 8 Earth years, since 8 × 8 = 64 = 4 × 4 × 4. Farther planets go round more slowly, in a longer orbit and at a lower speed. If the law looks like magic, the Toolkit page [Ratios and scaling](/toolkit/ratios-and-scaling/) shows how square and cube laws work and how to read them off a chart.

On a chart of period against distance, with both axes stretched by powers of ten, the law is a straight line, and everything that goes round the Sun is on it: the planets, the dwarf planets out to Eris, the comets. Kepler only had the six planets known in his day, but the law holds for bodies he never knew about, out to hundreds of times their distance.

Jupiter’s four large moons, which Galileo had found nine years earlier, follow a law of the same shape: their squared periods are in step with their cubed distances from Jupiter. But they sit on a different line, higher up the chart: at any given distance, a moon takes about 32 times as long to go round Jupiter as a planet would take to go round the Sun. Kepler couldn’t explain that. Newton could: the number depends on the mass at the centre, and Chapter 7 uses it to weigh the Sun and Jupiter.

## Where will the planet be?

Kepler’s laws say where a planet is at any moment, in principle. In practice there is a catch. The second law tells you how much area has been swept since perihelion: just the fraction of the year that has passed. Turning that area back into a position on the ellipse can’t be done with a formula. This is Kepler’s equation, and it has no exact solution in ordinary terms.

Kepler’s own way out was to guess a position, work out how much area that guess sweeps, see how far off it is, and adjust. That is still exactly how it is done. A computer makes a guess, checks it, and improves it, again and again, until the error is smaller than anything that matters, and for an ordinary orbit that takes three or four rounds. Every planet in this course’s sky view is placed this way, thousands of times a second as you drag the time slider.

<Callout kind="know">

Kepler’s laws are empirical: rules found in the data, not explained by anything deeper. They were tested by prediction. Kepler’s *Rudolphine Tables* of 1627, built on them, forecast a transit of Mercury across the Sun for 7 November 1631, and Pierre Gassendi in Paris saw it, a few hours from the predicted time: the first transit of a planet anyone had watched.

</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Solving Kepler’s equation">

Measure time round the orbit as an angle, the **mean anomaly** M: it grows steadily from 0 at perihelion to 360° a full orbit later. Measure position by another angle, the **eccentric anomaly** E: draw a circle round the ellipse, touching it at both ends, and take the point on the circle straight above or below the planet; E is that point’s angle at the centre. The second law then becomes Kepler’s equation:

M = E − e × sin E

where e is the eccentricity. Given E, finding M is easy. Given M, which is what we have when we know the time, finding E is the hard direction.

Newton’s method makes it routine. Start with a guess for E, for example E = M. Work out how far E − e sin E is from M: that is the miss. The slope of the curve at the guess is 1 − e cos E. Slide down the slope to where it would reach zero, and that is the next guess. Each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits, so a guess good to 1% becomes good to 0.01%, then to 0.000 001%.
