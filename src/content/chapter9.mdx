For most of history the stars were lights on a dome, all at the same unknown distance. Some Greek thinkers guessed that they were suns, very far away, and Giordano Bruno said it again in the 1580s. But nobody could prove it until someone measured how far away a star is, and then worked out how bright it must really be to look as it does from there. This chapter makes that measurement, meets our neighbours, and then steps back to see where the Sun sits in the Milky Way.

## How far is a star?

Hold up a thumb at arm’s length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the background. That shift is <Term id="parallax">parallax</Term>, and the farther away the thumb, the smaller the jump. Measure the jump and the distance between your eyes, and you can work out how far away the thumb is.

For the stars, the two eyes are Earth on opposite sides of its orbit, 300 million km apart. Look at a nearby star in January and again in July, and it shifts slightly against much more distant stars behind it. Over a year it traces a tiny ellipse. The shift is so small that it defeated astronomers for two thousand years. Its absence was even an argument against Earth moving at all (Chapter 5): if Earth goes round the Sun, why don’t the stars shift? The answer is that they do, but by less than a thousandth of a degree.

<Callout kind="key">

**Distance in parsecs = 1 ÷ parallax in arcseconds.** A star whose parallax is one <Term id="arcsecond">arcsecond</Term> is one <Term id="parsec">parsec</Term> away: 206,265 times the Earth–Sun distance, or 3.26 light-years. No star is that close.

</Callout>

In 1838 Friedrich Bessel announced the first parallax: 0.314 arcseconds for 61 Cygni, a faint double star he chose because it moves unusually fast across the sky, a hint that it is near. (The modern value is 0.286″, which puts it 11.4 light-years away.) At about the same time Thomas Henderson measured α Centauri and Friedrich Struve measured Vega. The stars were suns, at distances of millions of times the Earth–Sun distance.

Today the Gaia spacecraft, launched by the European Space Agency in December 2013, measures parallaxes from space to a few hundred-thousandths of an arcsecond. Its third data release, in 2022, gave parallaxes for about 1.5 billion stars. <GaiaDr4 /> Parallax is the first rung of every distance in astronomy: the farther methods of Part II are all calibrated by it.

## Our neighbours

Within 10 parsecs of the Sun (33 light-years) there are about 375 known stars, in some 340 systems: singles, pairs and triples. The figure shows the 323 in the HYG catalogue, where they really are.

The striking thing is who our neighbours are. About two thirds are <Term id="red-dwarf">red dwarfs</Term>: small, cool stars with a tenth to half the Sun’s mass, so faint that not one of them can be seen with the eye alone. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star of all at 4.25 light-years, is one, with at least one planet. There are about twenty <Term id="white-dwarf">white dwarfs</Term>, the Earth-sized cores left by stars that have died, and more than eighty <Term id="brown-dwarf">brown dwarfs</Term>, too small to shine as stars, most found only in the last thirty years by infrared telescopes. Only a handful of stars are bigger and brighter than the Sun: Sirius, Procyon, Altair, Vega, Fomalhaut. The Sun outshines more than nine in ten of its neighbours.

So why does the night sky look full of bright stars? Because the bright stars you see are mostly not our neighbours. They are the rare, powerful ones, visible from hundreds of light-years away, while the common faint ones nearby are invisible. A census of the eye’s stars is a census of the brightest stars in a huge volume, not of what is typical.

## The Sun from far away

How bright a star looks depends on how much light it gives out and how far away it is. Brightness falls with the square of the distance (Chapter 7): twice as far, a quarter of the light; ten times as far, a hundredth. On the astronomers’ <Term id="magnitude">magnitude</Term> scale, where bigger numbers are fainter, a hundredth of the light is five magnitudes. So knowing a star’s distance turns how bright it looks into how bright it is: its <Term id="absolute-magnitude">absolute magnitude</Term>, the brightness it would have seen from a standard 10 parsecs.

From α Centauri, the Sun would be a bright star of magnitude 0.5, one of the brightest in their sky, sitting in our constellation Cassiopeia (it would turn its W into a zigzag of six). From Vega it would be an ordinary faint star. And beyond about 17 parsecs, 55 light-years, it would fade below what the eye can see at all. Most of the stars that make up the constellations are farther than that, and outshine the Sun.

## Our place in the Milky Way

On a dark night, away from towns, a pale band of light crosses the sky: the Milky Way. In 1610 Galileo turned his telescope on it and found that it was made of “innumerable stars”. It is our own <Term id="galaxy">galaxy</Term>, seen from inside: a flat disc of stars, and we are in it, so looking along the disc we see stars piled on stars, and looking out of it we see few.

In 1785 William Herschel counted stars in hundreds of directions and concluded that the Galaxy is a flattened disc, with the Sun near its middle. He was misled: dust between the stars hides the distant ones, so in every direction along the disc he ran out of stars at about the same distance. In 1918 Harlow Shapley mapped the <Term id="globular-cluster">globular clusters</Term>, great balls of old stars that orbit the Galaxy above and below the dust, and found them centred on a point far away in Sagittarius. The Sun was moved off-centre, as Earth had been by Copernicus. (Shapley’s distances were too big, because he too didn’t know about the dust; Robert Trumpler showed in 1930 how much it dims distant stars.)

Today’s picture: the Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy about 100,000 light-years across, with a few hundred billion stars. The Sun is about 26,700 light-years from the centre, in a minor spiral arm, and it goes round at about 230 km/s, taking about 230 million years for each lap. At the centre sits a black hole four million times the Sun’s mass, which comes in Part III.

<DeepDive title="Why didn’t the ancients see parallax?">

They looked. Aristotle used its absence as an argument that Earth stands still, and Aristarchus, who put the Sun in the middle, could only answer that the stars must be immensely far away, which was right. The largest stellar parallax, Proxima Centauri’s 0.77″, is less than a seventieth of the finest detail the eye can see (about 1′), and Tycho Brahe’s best instruments measured to about 1′ too. It took telescopes, precise micrometers and Bessel’s patience, over a year of nightly measurements, to find it.

</DeepDive>
