On Christmas night 1758, Johann Palitzsch, a farmer near Dresden, found a faint smudge in his telescope. Fifty-three years earlier, Edmond Halley had predicted that a comet would come back about then, and he had died sixteen years before it did. The comet was right on time. The sky had become a machine you could calculate.

For thousands of years the sky has run like clockwork: the stars wheel overhead, the Moon and planets keep their schedules, and comets come back when gravity says they will. Part I takes that clockwork apart.

But the points of light on the dome are suns. They are born from collapsing gas, shine by fusion, and die as white dwarfs, neutron stars or black holes. Part II shows how we learned all that from starlight alone. Part III opens the machinery inside, down to the physics that decides each star's fate.
